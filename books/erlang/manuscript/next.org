* Where the book goes next

We have now crossed two boundaries beyond the basic supervision tree. Chapter 13 gave the
worker service an OTP application lifecycle, and Chapter 14 composed exact application/runtime
versions into a release with executable boot artifacts. Chapter 15 then made load ownership
explicit: Project 3 accepts only bounded work, keeps admitted-but-not-running jobs in an
application-level FIFO, starts temporary workers dynamically, and owns logical retry separately
from generic supervisor restart policy.

The failure lab at the end of Chapter 15 is the important constraint on everything that
follows. Killing the queue process causes its supervisor to restore the process role, but the
replacement process has no copy of the volatile job ledger. A sibling worker can still be
alive while the process that knew why it existed is gone. That is the exact point at which
"let it crash" stops being a complete recovery story.

Before moving outward, the reader should be able to answer these questions from mechanism:

1. Why is a =gen_server= mailbox not automatically a bounded application work queue?
2. At what point does =submit/1= transfer ownership of a job to Project 3?
3. Why are one-shot worker children =temporary= even though a logical job may be retried?
4. Which process owns the retry budget and stable job identity, and why is that not generic
   supervisor policy?
5. Why does starting a monitor before sending =run= close a local startup race?
6. What exactly is restored when =job_processor= is restarted by its supervisor?
7. Which accepted-work guarantees still require durable storage, replay, and idempotency?

The next major boundary is distribution. We will take process identity, message passing,
links/monitors, and supervision assumptions that were local to one BEAM and ask what changes
when cooperating Erlang nodes can connect, disconnect, and disagree about reachability.
Distribution will be taught as another failure model, not as transparent magic.

After that the book returns to the volatile-ledger problem with an explicit persistence and
recovery contract, then adds tracing/observability, Common Test system scenarios, measured
mailbox/backpressure experiments, networking boundaries, release operations, and production
debugging. None of those chapters may retroactively turn an in-memory test into a durability,
availability, or performance claim.

The discipline stays the same: every new layer must state what contract it adds, what
failure it can observe or recover, what state it owns, what evidence has actually been run,
and which uncertainty remains.
