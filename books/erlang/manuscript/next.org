* Where the book goes next

We now have the low-level failure vocabulary needed to stop treating OTP as magic. Project
1 has explicit request/reply protocols, timeouts that do not pretend to be cancellation,
monitor-based failure observation, link-based shared fate, exit trapping, and controlled
failure injection. The next chapters ask what changes when those mechanics are given
standard ownership and lifecycle contracts.

Chapter 10 introduces OTP design principles as conventions around process ownership and
system structure. Chapter 11 converts the messaging service into Project 2's
=gen_server=-based worker service, including callback boundaries, synchronous and
asynchronous requests, system messages, termination, and testing. Chapter 12 then adds a
real supervisor and makes restart strategy/intensity executable rather than rhetorical.
Applications and releases follow only after those local lifecycle boundaries are concrete.

Before moving on, the reader should be able to answer these questions without hand-waving:

1. Why is a link a different architectural claim from a monitor?
2. Which facts determine whether an incoming exit signal is dropped, terminates a process,
   or becomes an =EXIT= message?
3. Why does =process_flag(trap_exit, true)= not make a process a supervisor?
4. What does =demonitor(Ref, [flush])= protect the caller mailbox from after a request
   finishes?
5. Why can starting a replacement process restore availability while still losing all of
   the old process's in-memory state?
6. Which failures in Project 1 are observed, which propagate automatically, and which have
   no recovery policy yet?

Those questions become the checklist for OTP. We will keep the manual implementation as a
small executable reference, then replace ad-hoc lifecycle machinery with standard
behaviours one boundary at a time. The point is not that OTP hides processes. The point is
that it gives recurring process relationships names, callback contracts, ownership, and
well-tested operational policy.

Later projects continue outward: applications/releases, distributed Erlang and connection
failure, explicit persistence/recovery boundaries, Common Test, tracing and runtime
observability, mailbox/backpressure experiments, and production debugging. Each layer must
state what it adds and what failure uncertainty remains.
