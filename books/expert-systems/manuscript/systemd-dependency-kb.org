* Project 2 -- A Source-Backed systemd Dependency Diagnosis Knowledge Base
:PROPERTIES:
:ID:       1f81b922-ca4b-4272-96e8-e9b4d674d9e6
:END:

Project 1 built the machinery. Project 2 has a nastier job: put technical knowledge into that machinery without turning documentation fragments and operator folklore into fake certainty.

Our first real knowledge-base slice is intentionally narrow. We are going to model one piece of systemd dependency behavior well enough to answer a few precise questions:

- What is the difference between a requirement dependency and an ordering dependency?
- Under what documented conditions can a failed required unit prevent another unit from starting?
- Why is a failed =Wants== unit a different case?
- How do we keep evidence from different activation attempts or configuration generations from joining accidentally?
- Can an explanation show both what we observed and why a rule exists?

This is a better starting point than a hundred shallow troubleshooting rules.

The lesson is not “memorize systemd.” The lesson is how to engineer a rule base whose claims remain inspectable when the source system is subtle.

The upstream source for this milestone is pinned systemd v261.3, published on 2026-09-10. The dependency semantics come from =systemd.unit(5)= and the collection boundary comes from =systemctl(1)= [cite:@systemd-unit-v2613;@systemctl-v2613].

The observations in this chapter are *synthetic*. They do not describe a real machine. That separation is non-negotiable:

#+begin_example
upstream documentation -> justifies rule semantics
fixture/collector data  -> supplies observations
rule firing             -> derives a bounded hypothesis
explanation graph       -> preserves both lineages
#+end_example

If you blur those four layers, you can still build an engine that passes tests. You just cannot trust what it says.

The first Project 1 fixture used this fact:

#+begin_src lisp
(depends-on frontend api)
#+end_src

That was fine while we were teaching joins. It is not fine as an operational knowledge model.

“Depends on” can mean too many things: start this other unit too; order startup after it; stop when it is stopped; fail if it is unavailable; prefer it but continue without it; consume a service it happens to provide; wait for a resource condition; or merely mention it in configuration.

A rule engine cannot repair an ontology that erased the distinction before inference began.

** P2.1 Requirement is not ordering
In systemd v261.3, =Wants== and =Requires== are requirement dependencies, while =Before== and =After== are ordering dependencies. The manual explicitly says the ordering settings are independent of and orthogonal to requirement dependencies [cite:@systemd-unit-v2613].

So Project 2 uses two separate normalized relations:

#+begin_src lisp
(unit-requirement UNIT DEPENDENCY KIND GENERATION)
(unit-ordering    UNIT OTHER      DIRECTION GENERATION)
#+end_src

For this milestone:

#+begin_example
KIND      = :requires | :wants
DIRECTION = :after
#+end_example

We are not claiming those are systemd's internal data structures. They are the book KB's normalized relations.

Suppose the configuration contains:

#+begin_example
Requires=api.service
#+end_example

A common but wrong mental shortcut is:

#+begin_quote
frontend requires API, therefore API must finish starting before frontend starts.
#+end_quote

The upstream manual does not say that. Requirement dependencies do not determine order. =Before==/=After== do [cite:@systemd-unit-v2613].

That means this fact:

#+begin_src lisp
(unit-requirement "frontend.service"
                  "api.service"
                  :requires
                  "generation-1")
#+end_src

must not secretly imply this fact:

#+begin_src lisp
(unit-ordering "frontend.service"
               "api.service"
               :after
               "generation-1")
#+end_src

The KB needs both when a rule depends on both.

The inverse error is just as bad. =After=api.service= orders the two units *if they are both part of the transaction*. Ordering alone does not say “pull =api.service= into this transaction.”

These near misses are different:

#+begin_example
Requires=api.service             After=api.service
(no After=)                      (no Wants=/Requires=)

requirement known                ordering known
ordering not proved              pull-in not proved
#+end_example

An expert system that collapses both into =depends-on= cannot tell you which piece of evidence is missing.

** P2.2 The first source-backed diagnostic rule
The v261.3 =Requires== documentation says that when the configuring unit is activated, required units are activated too. If one of those required units fails to activate *and an =After== ordering dependency on that failing unit is set*, the configuring unit is not started [cite:@systemd-unit-v2613].

We can encode exactly that bounded claim.

The rule's left-hand side is:

#+begin_src lisp
((unit-requirement ?unit ?dependency :requires ?generation)
 (unit-ordering ?unit ?dependency :after ?generation)
 (activation-attempt ?unit ?attempt ?generation)
 (activation-outcome ?dependency ?attempt ?generation :failed))
#+end_src

And its conclusion is shaped like:

#+begin_src lisp
(diagnostic-hypothesis
 :required-dependency-blocked-startup
 ?unit ?dependency ?attempt ?generation)
#+end_src

Notice how much is *not* in that rule. It does not say every failure of =?unit= is caused by =?dependency=, that =Requires== keeps the dependency active forever, that every dependency is =Requires==, that a journal message proves an activation outcome, or that a failure from another attempt is reusable.

The conclusion is named =diagnostic-hypothesis= on purpose. The rule is source-backed, but the observations in the book fixture are synthetic and the KB is not a complete model of every reason a unit may fail to start.

** P2.3 Context belongs in facts, not in our imagination
Early expert-system examples often look timeless:

#+begin_example
(service-failed api)
#+end_example

Real operations are not timeless. The API can fail in one attempt and succeed in the next. Configuration can change between deployments. A dependency edge can disappear. Two facts with the same nouns can describe different worlds.

This milestone uses two explicit context fields:

#+begin_example
ATTEMPT
GENERATION
#+end_example

The normalized evidence is:

#+begin_src lisp
(activation-attempt UNIT ATTEMPT GENERATION)
(activation-outcome UNIT ATTEMPT GENERATION OUTCOME)
#+end_src

The configuration facts also carry =GENERATION=.

This gives ordinary unification/join logic enough information to reject bad combinations. We do not need a special temporal-reasoning engine merely to avoid mixing records that clearly say they came from different contexts.

Consider this broken evidence set:

#+begin_example
unit-requirement   frontend api :requires generation-0
unit-ordering      frontend api :after    generation-0
activation-attempt frontend attempt-7     generation-1
activation-outcome api      attempt-7     generation-1 :failed
#+end_example

The rule cannot bind one value for =?generation= across all four conditions. No diagnosis is produced.

*** Failure lab -- delete the generation key
Make a copy of the rule and remove =?generation= from the configuration relations. Feed it configuration from generation 0 and observations from generation 1.

Ask:

1. Does the rule fire?
2. Which exact evidence says the old dependency declaration still applies?
3. Can the explanation graph reveal the semantic bug, or does it merely explain the wrong rule faithfully?

The third question is the important one. Explanation cannot rescue a bad model. It can only make the model's mistake visible.

** P2.4 Observation provenance is not rule provenance
Project 1 gave every fact and production a provenance slot. Project 2 makes those slots earn their keep.

There are two different provenance questions.

*** Where did this observation come from?
The fixture might say:

#+begin_src lisp
(:kind :synthetic-observation
 :status :synthetic
 :scenario "systemd-dependency-fixture"
 :source-id "fixture:dependency-outcome"
 :source "book-fixture")
#+end_src

A real collector would substitute capture-specific identity: host/tenant, collector version, capture time, boot or activation identity, configuration revision, property name/API endpoint, and a raw artifact reference or hash.

Do not replace all of that with “source: systemd docs.” The documentation did not observe your host.

*** Why does this rule exist?
The production provenance instead pins the semantic authority:

#+begin_src lisp
(:kind :upstream-documentation
 :project "systemd"
 :release "v261.3"
 :published "2026-09-10"
 :retrieved "2026-09-16"
 :path "man/systemd.unit.xml"
 :url "https://github.com/systemd/systemd/blob/v261.3/man/systemd.unit.xml"
 :documented-claim
 :requires-plus-after-and-failed-required-unit-prevents-dependent-start
 :course-status :source-backed-teaching-rule)
#+end_src

Now an explanation can answer two independent questions:

#+begin_example
Why do we believe the dependency failed in this modeled attempt?
  -> observation provenance

Why is that failure + Requires + After relevant to startup?
  -> production provenance
#+end_example

That is the beginning of a real technical knowledge system.

** P2.5 Do not parse =systemctl status= because it looks convenient
Sooner or later somebody will propose this collector:

#+begin_src sh
systemctl status api.service | grep failed
#+end_src

Do not build the knowledge base on that boundary.

The v261.3 =systemctl= manual explicitly says =status= is intended to generate human-readable output. It directs programs that want computer-parsable output to =show= instead [cite:@systemctl-v2613]. It also says =status= presents runtime information for the current or most recent retained invocation and includes recent journal data; earlier invocations or previous boots belong in journal queries [cite:@systemctl-v2613].

This creates three architecture rules for later collectors:

1. Prefer documented machine-oriented properties to human display text.
2. Keep current runtime properties distinct from historical journal evidence.
3. Attach context identity before normalized observations enter reasoning.

This milestone implements none of those collectors. The executable project starts *after* that boundary, with normalized synthetic facts. That is an honest interface rather than fake integration.

** P2.6 Wants= is not a weaker score for Requires=
A bad implementation might encode:

#+begin_example
Requires -> confidence 1.0
Wants    -> confidence 0.5
#+end_example

That is nonsense. The difference is semantic, not a confidence scalar.

The v261.3 manual says =Wants== is a weak requirement dependency and that failure of a wanted unit does not invalidate the transaction as a whole [cite:@systemd-unit-v2613]. Therefore this milestone gives =Wants== its own rule and conclusion type.

Its left side is:

#+begin_src lisp
((unit-requirement ?unit ?dependency :wants ?generation)
 (activation-attempt ?unit ?attempt ?generation)
 (activation-outcome ?dependency ?attempt ?generation :failed))
#+end_src

Its conclusion is:

#+begin_src lisp
(diagnostic-boundary
 :wanted-unit-failure-alone-does-not-invalidate-transaction
 ?unit ?dependency ?attempt ?generation)
#+end_src

That is not a happy-path diagnosis. It is a guardrail against the wrong diagnosis.

This is an important expert-system pattern: represent a source-backed exception or boundary explicitly when it prevents a common overgeneralization.

** P2.7 Build the KB as a consumer of the engine
The Project 2 module lives separately from the inference engine.

#+begin_example
books/expert-systems/code/
  inference-engine/
    matcher.lisp
    engine.lisp
    incremental.lisp
    truth-maintenance.lisp
    assumptions.lisp
    explanation.lisp

  service-kb/
    package.lisp
    systemd-dependencies.lisp
    tests.lisp
    run-tests.lisp
#+end_example

The KB is allowed to construct facts and productions, construct the accepted truth-maintained/assumption network, run it, and ask the accepted explanation API for a result.

It is not allowed to reach into beta-memory internals to force a diagnosis, write justification records manually, forge explanation nodes, redefine truth-maintenance semantics, or special-case systemd inside the matcher.

The domain layer must prove that the general mechanisms are useful by using them as designed.

** P2.8 A complete worked case
Take this synthetic case:

#+begin_example
Configuration generation: generation-1
Activation attempt:       attempt-1

frontend.service Requires=api.service
frontend.service After=api.service
api.service failed during attempt-1
frontend.service is the modeled unit being activated in attempt-1
#+end_example

The normalized facts are equivalent to:

#+begin_src lisp
(unit-requirement
 "frontend.service" "api.service" :requires "generation-1")

(unit-ordering
 "frontend.service" "api.service" :after "generation-1")

(activation-attempt
 "frontend.service" "attempt-1" "generation-1")

(activation-outcome
 "api.service" "attempt-1" "generation-1" :failed)
#+end_src

Trace the join.

*** Condition 1 -- requirement
Bindings after the requirement fact:

#+begin_example
?unit       = "frontend.service"
?dependency = "api.service"
?generation = "generation-1"
#+end_example

*** Condition 2 -- ordering
The =unit=, =dependency=, and =generation= fields must agree with the existing environment. If the ordering fact names another generation, the partial match dies here.

*** Condition 3 -- activation attempt
This binds =?attempt= to ="attempt-1"= and rechecks the unit/generation context.

*** Condition 4 -- dependency outcome
The dependency, attempt, generation, and literal =:failed= outcome all have to match.

Only then does the production propose a derived fact.

The final fact ID is deterministic from the modeled identity:

#+begin_example
hypothesis.required-dependency-blocked.frontend.service.api.service.attempt-1.generation-1
#+end_example

Stable identity makes regression tests, explanation queries, and later persistence/versioning work easier.

** P2.9 The explanation should be a graph, not a paragraph
After the production fires, call the already-built explanation layer on the hypothesis ID.

The expected *shape* is:

#+begin_example
hypothesis fact occurrence
  |
  +--supported-by--> justification
                         |
                         +-- production-id
                         +-- systemd v261.3 rule provenance
                         +-- exact bindings
                         |
                         +--uses--> requirement observation
                         +--uses--> ordering observation
                         +--uses--> activation-attempt observation
                         +--uses--> dependency-outcome observation
#+end_example

Each support fact in turn has its own source node carrying the synthetic observation provenance.

This is why Project 1 invested in exact occurrence identity before we wrote a real KB. If a premise is retracted and the hypothesis loses its final grounded justification, truth maintenance can remove it. If the same stable premise ID later reappears under a new occurrence stamp, the old explanation cannot silently attach itself to the new evidence.

** P2.10 Failure lab -- Requires= without After=
Construct the same fixture but omit the ordering fact.

You still know:

#+begin_example
frontend requires api
api failed in this modeled attempt
#+end_example

But this milestone's source-backed blocked-startup rule must not fire because the documented condition it models includes =After==.

Query the expected hypothesis ID anyway. The correct explanation status is =:absent=.

Be careful with the sentence you attach to that result.

Wrong:

#+begin_quote
The dependency did not block frontend startup.
#+end_quote

Better:

#+begin_quote
This KB did not derive the =Requires + After + failed required unit= startup-blocked hypothesis from the current modeled evidence.
#+end_quote

Absence of one diagnosis is not proof of the opposite.

** P2.11 Failure lab -- After= without a requirement edge
Keep the =After== fact but omit =Requires=/=Wants==.

Ordering tells us when startup happens relative to another unit *if both units are involved*. It does not establish that the other unit is pulled into the transaction [cite:@systemd-unit-v2613].

Again the blocked-startup hypothesis remains absent.

This lab is useful because the surface words sound causal:

#+begin_example
frontend is AFTER api
api FAILED
#+end_example

Humans are tempted to tell a story from that. The production is deliberately less imaginative.

** P2.12 Failure lab -- stale configuration generation
Use:

#+begin_example
Requires/After -> generation-0
activation attempt/outcome -> generation-1
#+end_example

The join must fail.

This is not a minor data-cleaning rule. It is the difference between reasoning about a configuration that existed and a configuration that exists in the modeled event.

Extend the lab:

1. Make =generation= a content hash instead of a friendly name.
2. Explain which collector stage should assign it.
3. Decide whether two semantically equivalent but byte-different configurations should share a generation.
4. Write down the trust boundary. Do not solve it by deleting the field.

** P2.13 Failure lab -- stale activation attempt
Keep configuration constant but set:

#+begin_example
frontend activation attempt = attempt-1
api failure observation     = attempt-0
#+end_example

A human looking at a dashboard may still see “API failed recently.” The KB must not borrow that failure into the current attempt unless a separate rule explicitly establishes that relevance.

This is provenance-aware temporal hygiene without pretending we have built a temporal logic.

** P2.14 Debugging the rule in five questions
When the expected hypothesis is absent, do not immediately add another rule. Walk the boundary.

*** 1. Is the requirement kind correct?
Inspect the normalized =unit-requirement= fact. =:wants= and =:requires= are not confidence levels of one relation.

*** 2. Is an ordering fact present?
The positive rule requires =:after=. Do not infer it from =Requires==.

*** 3. Do unit/dependency identities agree?
A typo or alias-normalization failure can kill the join. Identity normalization belongs in the collector/schema boundary, not in ad-hoc matcher hacks.

*** 4. Do attempt and generation agree?
Print the bindings or the exact facts. If the context differs, the failed join may be correct.

*** 5. Is =:failed= actually the normalized outcome?
This milestone deliberately does not interpret systemd =Result== values or parse arbitrary journal strings. If your collector produced some other outcome vocabulary, the contract needs explicit research and an adapter.

Those five questions are more useful than “why didn't RETE fire?” because they operate at the knowledge boundary first.

** P2.15 Debugging a wrong positive diagnosis
A false-looking positive is more serious. Check in this order:

1. *Exact supports.* Which four occurrence stamps grounded the justification?
2. *Observation provenance.* Are any supports synthetic, stale, wrong-host, wrong-tenant, or wrong-boot?
3. *Bindings.* Did =?unit=, =?dependency=, =?attempt=, and =?generation= bind to what you think they did?
4. *Production identity.* Which stable rule ID fired?
5. *Production provenance.* Which pinned source and documented claim justify that rule?
6. *Claim boundary.* Is the UI presenting a bounded hypothesis as causal certainty?
7. *Model coverage.* Is a missing systemd semantic being silently treated as impossible?

The explanation graph makes 1--5 inspectable. Good application design must preserve 6--7.

** P2.16 What “source-backed” does and does not mean
A source-backed rule is not automatically a good operational recommendation.

It means the rule's semantic premise was traced to an inspected authoritative source, the source version is pinned, the book records what claim was derived from the source, the executable rule is narrower than or equal to that claim, and tests exercise the intended boundary.

It does not mean our normalized observations are correct, our collector is complete, the upstream documentation covers every runtime corner case, the hypothesis has a probability attached to it, the hypothesis is the only possible diagnosis, or the KB has been evaluated on production incidents.

A serious expert system keeps those claims apart.

** P2.17 Why this is already better than a pile of shell if-statements
You could encode the happy path as shell:

#+begin_src sh
if requires_api && after_api && api_failed; then
    echo "api blocked frontend"
fi
#+end_src

The value of the expert-system architecture is not the =if=.

It is the surrounding semantics: stable evidence identities, explicit context keys, variable-consistent joins, source-backed stable rule identity, deterministic activations, truth-maintained derived conclusions, support-sensitive retraction, contradiction/counterevidence infrastructure, explanation graphs that preserve observation and rule provenance, and a future workbench that can inspect partial matches and version rule changes.

The rule body is intentionally simple. The knowledge lifecycle is the hard part.

** P2.18 Project lab -- extend the dependency vocabulary without lying
Pick *one* new systemd dependency type from the current pinned manual. Good candidates for later research include =BindsTo==, =Requisite==, or =PartOf==.

Do not code first. Produce this table:

| Field | Required answer |
|-
| Pinned upstream source | exact release/path/section |
| Documented semantics | narrow paraphrase |
| What it does *not* imply | at least two boundaries |
| Normalized relation | proposed fact shape |
| Context key | attempt/generation/etc. |
| Positive scenario | explicit facts |
| Near miss | explicit facts |
| Counterevidence | what could weaken the operational conclusion? |
| Rule provenance | stable ID + source metadata |
| Test oracle | exact expected fact/explanation status |

Only then implement the rule. If you cannot fill the table without hand-waving, the rule is not ready.

** P2.19 Exercises
*** ★ 1 -- Requirement versus ordering
In your own words, explain why =Requires=api.service= does not by itself prove that =api.service= finishes startup before the configuring unit begins.

*** ★ 2 -- Pull-in versus ordering
Explain why =After=api.service= is not equivalent to =Wants=api.service=.

*** ★ 3 -- Provenance separation
Given an observation fact and a production, list the provenance fields that belong to the observation and the ones that belong to the rule. Explain why the upstream manual URL should not be used as the observation source.

*** ★ 4 -- Absence language
Write two UI messages for an absent blocked-startup hypothesis: one misleading and one precise.

*** ★★ 5 -- Attempt isolation
Extend the fixture with two activation attempts. Make attempt 0 fail and attempt 1 succeed. Prove with a test that attempt 0 cannot support the attempt-1 hypothesis.

*** ★★ 6 -- Configuration revision
Replace the friendly generation string with a deterministic fixture hash. Add a regression in which only the ordering relation comes from the older generation.

*** ★★ 7 -- Explain the =Wants== boundary
Query the derived =diagnostic-boundary= fact for a failed wanted unit. Walk its explanation graph and identify the exact observation sources and the production's pinned documentation provenance.

*** ★★ 8 -- Property boundary
Read the pinned =systemctl(1)= =show= documentation. Design a collector record for only the properties you actually need. Do not parse =status= output. Mark every property whose semantics still require separate source research.

*** ★★★ 9 -- Partial-match debugger design
Without implementing negative conditional elements, design a workbench query that can say:

#+begin_example
rule.systemd.requires-after-failed-dependency
  matched requirement: yes
  matched ordering:    no
  matched attempt:     yes
  matched outcome:     yes
#+end_example

Specify what additional matcher/network inspection API you would need. Do not infer “ordering absent from reality” from “no matching fact in this KB.”

*** ★★★ 10 -- Counterevidence model
Design an explicit counterevidence fact that could challenge the operational usefulness of a blocked-startup hypothesis without contradicting the source semantics—for example, evidence that the modeled attempt identity is wrong. Decide whether it belongs in contradiction machinery, diagnostic ranking, or data-quality validation, and defend the choice.

*** ★★★ 11 -- Source upgrade
Assume systemd v262 changes wording around one dependency directive. Describe the review procedure before changing the rule. Include source diff, regression replay, provenance versioning, and what happens to historical explanations that cite v261.3.

*** ★★★ 12 -- Do not invent uncertainty
A teammate proposes assigning 0.9 confidence to =Requires== diagnoses and 0.6 to =Wants== diagnoses. Explain why that is semantically wrong. Propose what empirical or model-based work would actually be required before introducing calibrated uncertainty.

** P2.20 Checkpoint boundary
This milestone establishes a separated Project 2 domain module consuming the existing engine; source-backed systemd v261.3 dependency rules; explicit requirement versus ordering relations; activation-attempt/configuration-generation identity; synthetic observation provenance separate from rule/source provenance; one bounded blocked-startup hypothesis; one explicit =Wants== boundary fact; executable near-miss scenarios; and explanation reuse across the domain boundary.

It does *not* establish a real systemd collector, a parser for =systemctl status=, =Result== semantics, journal parsing, complete unit transaction reconstruction, all systemd dependency types, production incident accuracy, uncertainty scoring, database persistence, concurrent rule updates, or a benchmark.

That is exactly how a serious knowledge base should grow: one sourced semantic boundary at a time, with runnable failures around it.
