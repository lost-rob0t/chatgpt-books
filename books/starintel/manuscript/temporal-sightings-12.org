* Chapter -- Multiple Sightings over Time
:PROPERTIES:
:ID:       b93cd156-6ccf-49af-8975-2c1c23b4c49b
:END:

A single sighting says that an instrument reported something. A sequence adds
time, but it does not automatically add a reliable track. Before drawing a line
between points, we need to know which equipment produced the observations,
where the receivers were, how their clocks relate, and which gaps the collection
system could have caused.

This chapter uses fictional, explicitly enrolled BLE beacons belonging to one
laboratory. Two fixed receivers and an owned receiver cart observe the equipment
in a controlled, invented workspace. The next chapter uses the same evidence to
describe equipment behavior. Neither chapter identifies people or infers their
private routines.

The actual hardware has not been selected. We assume dedicated fictional
beacons with a documented advertising configuration. We do not assume that a
phone, headset or other Bluetooth product advertises continuously, exposes the
same identifiers, or supports the same scanning features.

** State the measurement problem first

Our question is deliberately small:

#+begin_example
For an enrolled laboratory asset, can the supplied sightings support
a west-bay or east-bay classification during each evaluation window?

When does the evidence support a change of zone, and when must the answer
remain unknown?
#+end_example

This is a zone-classification exercise. It is not a claim that ordinary RSSI
measurements provide GPS-like coordinates. A zone answer can be useful for
finding owned equipment even when a defensible two-dimensional position is
unavailable.

Bluetooth advertising and scanning are separate activities, and advertising
broadcasts do not provide a receiver acknowledgement. A repeated payload can
appear on more than one advertising channel. Counting every received row as a
new equipment event would therefore overstate the evidence.
[cite:@bluetooth-primer-owned]

Write down the assumed beacon state, scan configuration, receiver software,
observation window and expected collection coverage. If these conditions are
unknown, a missing sighting is difficult to interpret.

** Keep four identities separate

The lab distinguishes:

1. Enrolled asset identity: the owned piece of equipment.
2. Transmitter identity or binding: the operator-approved relationship between
   that asset and its configured beacon.
3. Receiver identity: the instrument that recorded the sighting.
4. Observation identity: the particular receiver record being processed.

A radio address is an observed transport attribute. It is not a durable
inventory identity. Bluetooth supports private addressing, so an address can
change. The official privacy guidance treats private addresses as a privacy
mechanism, not an invitation to reconstruct identities across unrelated
observations. [cite:@bluetooth-privacy-owned]

The exercise's enrollment registry is trusted input supplied by the fictional
operator. A time-bounded binding explicitly associates a known address with an
enrolled beacon for that experiment. An unknown address remains unresolved;
similar signal strength, a matching name or temporal proximity does not create
a new binding.

This registry is also not a cryptographic authentication claim. An identifier
in a received payload can be copied. The lab's duplicate and replay tests
concern repeated records in the supplied dataset; they do not establish
over-the-air replay protection for generic Bluetooth advertisements.

** A receiver coordinate is instrument context

Our fixed receivers occupy local positions at the west and east ends of an
invented twenty-metre workspace. The cart has separately recorded receiver
poses at designated stops.

A sighting should retain the receiver pose that was applicable when the
measurement was made. It must not inherit the cart's latest pose merely because
the record was ingested later.

For example, suppose the cart records a beacon at the west stop and uploads
the record after moving to the east stop. Attaching the east-stop coordinate
to that old measurement changes the experimental geometry. Sorting records
correctly does not repair a wrong pose association.

Keep two statements separate:

#+begin_example
Receiver R observed an enrolled beacon while R was at pose P.

The beacon's position or zone is estimated as Q by method M,
using observations O and calibration C.
#+end_example

The first is instrument context. The second is an inference with assumptions
and uncertainty. Copying P into Q produces a false location claim.

Our local coordinates are metres in a fictional room frame. They are not
latitude and longitude. If a real deployment later needs a geographic map, it
must define and validate the transformation between its local frame and the
map's coordinate reference system.

** Build a sighting record that can survive reprocessing

The raw teaching records preserve the information needed to revisit a result:

- the receiver and its observation identifier;
- the observed radio attribute and applicable enrollment binding;
- the receiver's acquisition clock reading;
- the clock correction and its uncertainty;
- the ingestion or receipt time;
- RSSI with its unit;
- the receiver pose and the interval for which that pose is valid;
- scan or coverage state;
- the calibration session and processing version used later.

Do not replace the raw measurement with the eventual zone label. A revised
calibration should be able to produce a new analysis from the same retained
observations.

The canonical StarIntel =observation= type can represent measurement content.
The common envelope supplies identities and provenance; fields such as
=observerId=, =subjectId=, =observedProperty=, =value=, =rawValue=, =unit= and
=method= provide additional vocabulary. Local coordinate and experiment-specific
structures remain explicitly documented payload content rather than invented
top-level schema fields. [cite:@starlang-starintel-0101]

A canonical observation fixture is included directly from the executable lab
so its published field names stay aligned with the tested example:

#+end_src

The example is synthetic. Its receiver context does not assert the beacon's
position, and its experiment payload does not introduce a new schema dtype.

** Reconcile clocks before comparing receivers

An acquisition timestamp and an ingestion timestamp answer different questions.
The first concerns the measurement; the second concerns the data pipeline.

Some real collector APIs expose an acquisition time on a receiver-local clock.
For example, Android's =ScanResult.getTimestampNanos()= reports time since boot.
It is not a UTC timestamp. =getRssi()= reports dBm, while transmit-power
information can be unavailable. [cite:@android-scanresult-owned]

The chapter's clock model is a teaching assumption:

#+begin_example
receiver reading = shared experiment time + receiver offset
corrected observation time = receiver reading - receiver offset
#+end_example

If receiver A reads 1005 with an offset of +5 seconds, its corrected time is
1000. If B reads 998 with an offset of -2 seconds, its corrected time is also
1000. Their records can arrive at different ingestion times without describing
different acquisition times.

A correction is an estimate, not a guarantee. Retain a bound or other justified
description of its uncertainty. If an observation is corrected to 1000 with a justified maximum clock-error
bound of one second, its bounded time interval is 999 to 1001 under that
model. A standard deviation or other statistical uncertainty is not itself a
hard error bound; preserve which interpretation was supplied.

Two overlapping time intervals do not establish a strict order. If one
observation's latest plausible time precedes another's earliest plausible time,
the ordering is supported by that model. Otherwise, retain the ambiguity.

Clock synchronization is its own engineering problem. NTP's specification
distinguishes offset, delay, dispersion and jitter; our supplied constant
offsets do not implement that protocol or establish its error guarantees.
[cite:@rfc5905-owned]

Most importantly, aligning receiver timestamps for grouping sightings is not
radio time-of-flight ranging. This lab does not measure propagation time.

** Remove duplicate records without erasing useful observations

Suppose a receiver record is delivered twice because an uploader retried.
Processing both copies should not double its influence.

The course data uses explicit receiver/sample identities so that this case is
deterministic. A repeated identity with the same content is a duplicate. A
repeated identity with different content is a conflict that deserves an
explanation, not a silently selected winner.

Different receivers observing the same transmission remain different
instrument observations. Conversely, a repeated advertisement at one receiver
does not necessarily represent a new asset movement. These are different
deduplication questions.

Preserve the reason a row was dropped or held aside. Useful counters include
raw rows, accepted unique observations, duplicate rows, conflicting identities,
unknown enrollment bindings and invalid measurements. Without those counters,
a changing pipeline can make equipment appear more or less active even when
only the ingestion behavior changed.

Do not describe dataset deduplication as a security guarantee. A copied beacon
payload with a new observation identity is not detected as a replay by a
receiver-record deduplicator.

** Why signal strength alone is not a ruler

RSSI is a measured signal-strength indicator. Position estimation introduces a
model connecting that measurement to geometry. Reference power, antennas,
orientation, obstructions, reflections and receiver characteristics can affect
the relationship.

The Bluetooth Core 6.0 overview explains the calibration needed for RSSI-based
distance estimation and its limitations. Ordinary RSSI, Direction Finding and
Channel Sounding are different measurement capabilities; the latter methods
must not be assumed simply because a device supports Bluetooth.
[cite:@bluetooth-core6-owned;@bluetooth-ranging-owned]

Here is a sensitivity example, not a measured result. Under a chosen
log-distance model with path-loss exponent two, a six-decibel error in the
relevant signal-strength difference changes the inferred distance by about a
factor of two. Changing the assumed exponent or environment changes the
relationship. More decimal places in the calculated distance do not remove
that model uncertainty.

Averaging may reduce some random variation. It does not automatically remove
a persistent bias from a metal obstruction or a changed receiver orientation.
Closely spaced packets can share the same bias and should not be treated as a
large collection of independent geometric constraints.

This is why the executable lab starts with calibrated zone fingerprints.
It asks whether the observed pattern resembles a supported zone under the
experiment's conditions. It leaves ambiguous and poorly supported windows
unclassified.

** Calibrate a deliberately small zone model

The fictional lab uses two zones, =west-bay= and =east-bay=. Calibration rows
come from separately labeled placements of owned equipment. Test rows are held
out from those calibration placements.

The course model uses median RSSI fingerprints and a conservative acceptance
policy. Its supplied thresholds require enough receiver support, sufficiently
recent and time-aligned observations, an acceptable residual from a known
fingerprint, and enough separation between the best two candidates.

These thresholds are exercise parameters. They are not universal Bluetooth
accuracy claims. A minimum receiver separation is a geometry heuristic here;
it does not prove measurement independence or make a two-receiver arrangement
a full two-dimensional ranging system.

The cart's calibrated stop is part of its measurement context. A fingerprint
learned with the cart at one stop must not be reused as though it applied at
every point on the cart's route.

Before testing, document:

- which labeled placements form calibration;
- which sessions are held out;
- the receiver and pose configuration;
- the signal preprocessing rule;
- the decision thresholds;
- what produces an unknown result;
- which quantities will be evaluated.

Changing the thresholds after inspecting the held-out answers turns that set
into development data. Use a separate final evaluation set if you tune on it.

** Form windows with explicit coverage

A window is a grouping rule, not an event observed by a radio. Record its
duration and how boundary cases are handled.

Each window needs two kinds of evidence:

1. Positive sightings from eligible, enrolled observations.
2. Coverage information about which receivers were expected and able to
   observe during that interval.

If a receiver was offline, its lack of sightings cannot be used as negative
evidence about the beacon. If a moving receiver's pose is stale, that receiver
may be unable to support the position model even though its radio is working.

A coverage-qualified empty window still requires careful wording: the beacon
was not observed by this configured collection process. That does not prove it
was absent, powered off, or outside a precisely known boundary.

Show missingness explicitly. A zero RSSI value is not a valid replacement for
a missing measurement, and carrying forward the last zone label indefinitely
makes a historical observation look current.

** Make transitions conservative

A single changed classification can be noise. The lab requires repeated
accepted support before recording a zone transition. This introduces a
tradeoff: fewer false transitions, but some delay in recognizing a genuine
change. Every selected observation identity in a confirming support set must
be new relative to earlier results for that asset. Reusing even one supporting
sample resets this lab's confirmation chain; adding one fresh row to an old
support set is not enough. Distinct observation identities avoid that duplicate-support
error, but they do not establish statistical independence of radio errors.

Unknown windows reset continuity. The sequence

#+begin_example
west-bay -> unknown interval -> east-bay
#+end_example

supports two separated observations. It does not prove the path taken between
them, the time of a boundary crossing, or continuous visibility.

Do not draw an uninterrupted track through a coverage gap. If a visualization
connects points for readability, make the unobserved segment visibly different
and label it as a gap rather than a measured route.

A smoothed line is another derived product. Its assumptions, input identities
and processing version belong with the result. Smoothing cannot create
observations in intervals where no eligible evidence exists.

** Separate observation, estimate and display age

The most recent accepted estimate has an age. A display should expose that age,
the supporting observations, and whether the relevant coverage and permission
conditions still apply.

An old estimate becoming stale does not mean the equipment has moved. A new
packet arriving late does not make its old acquisition time recent. A receiver
returning after an outage does not retroactively fill the outage.

This distinction also protects analysis from a common feedback loop: a UI
carries forward a zone, another process exports that display state as a fresh
observation, and the system then treats its own assumption as new evidence.
Derived estimates should retain their original observation times and lineage.

** Evaluate the claim you actually make

A zone classifier should be evaluated on zone labels. A metric position model
requires independent ground-truth coordinates before you can report
localization error.

For a hypothetical position estimate, the planar error in a known local frame
is the distance between the predicted and ground-truth coordinates at matched
times. Report a distribution such as median and a predeclared high percentile,
not only the best example. Also report how many cases produced no estimate.

For our zone baseline, useful outputs include:

- a confusion matrix on labeled held-out windows;
- accuracy among accepted classifications;
- the fraction of all predeclared evaluation windows receiving an accepted
  classification, with coverage reported separately;
- the rate of incorrect or unsupported transitions;
- transition-detection delay under a declared timing convention;
- stale, uncovered and unknown-window counts;
- acquisition-to-ingestion latency;
- duplicate and invalid-row rates.

A method that abstains almost everywhere may have high accepted-case accuracy
and poor utility. A method that always produces a zone may have high coverage
and unacceptable error. Show both.

** Worked reasoning checks

1. Two rows have the same receiver and sample identity after an upload retry.
   How much independent evidence was added?

   None by that repeated row. Keep one observation and record the duplicate
   handling. Investigate if their contents differ.

2. A moving receiver's latest coordinate is east, but the sighting was acquired
   earlier at its west stop. Which pose applies?

   The pose valid at acquisition time. If that association cannot be supported,
   exclude it from a geometry-dependent estimate or mark the result unknown.

3. Two receivers' corrected times differ by a small amount, but their clock
   uncertainties are larger than the allowed association interval. Are they
   safely simultaneous?

   Not under that rule. Keep the temporal uncertainty visible rather than
   treating nominal corrections as exact.

4. The east receiver is offline and reports no beacon. Does the west receiver
   alone prove a west-bay location?

   Not if the model requires both receivers. Coverage failure should reduce the
   available inference, not strengthen a convenient conclusion.

5. A new radio address appears with the same advertised name. Is it the same
   enrolled beacon?

   Unknown unless the approved enrollment process supplies the binding. The
   lab does not infer identity across unrelated address changes.

6. A zone changes after a long unobserved interval. Is the transition time known?

   No. Report separated supported states and the intervening gap. Do not invent
   a crossing time.

7. A distance formula prints 3.142 metres. Has the measurement acquired
   millimetre-level accuracy?

   No. Numerical formatting does not measure model error, receiver calibration
   error or environmental uncertainty.

** Project checkpoint

The companion lab in =code/owned-beacon-lab/= supplies the data, processing
rules and tests. Its README identifies the actual commands and parameter
values. Read the report alongside the raw and rejected-row counts before
interpreting a zone sequence.

controlled exercise; it does not certify unspecified hardware or a deployed
