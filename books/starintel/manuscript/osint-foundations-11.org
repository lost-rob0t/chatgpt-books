* Fieldwork -- OSINT Fundamentals and Location Evidence
:PROPERTIES:
:ID:       3d7b64f3-2f19-40c2-a8bd-4f09304cbd4d
:END:

A useful investigation ends with a supported answer to a defined question.
It also leaves enough evidence for another reader to understand the answer,
challenge it, and see what remains unknown. This chapter teaches that work:
turning an initial name or organization into a bounded research question,
finding relevant public material, resolving ambiguous identities, and assessing
what location evidence actually says.

Every person, organization, venue, source statement, date and location in the
worked cases is fictional. The search strings illustrate syntax; they are not
instructions to investigate someone with a similar real name. The location lab
uses invented venue drawings and an opt-in simulation feed. Its objective is
to evaluate evidence, not to infer a private person's current whereabouts.

The chapter connects to the investigation model you have just read. Research
intent belongs in a bounded plan; observations, claims and contradictions need
distinct identities; a diagram connecting two records is not proof that their
subjects are the same person.

** Begin with a question that can be answered

An instruction such as "find everything about Alex" has no useful completion
condition. It encourages collecting unrelated information and makes it hard
to recognize a wrong match. Replace it with a question whose scope and evidence
requirements are visible.

Our fictional assignment is:

#+begin_example
Which Alex Rivera is listed for the talk "River Sensors" in the Harbor Data
Forum programme, and which organization does that programme associate with
the speaker?

Separately, which fictional public venue is consistent with the supplied
venue drawing, and what time information is actually supported?
#+end_example

The permitted source set is public professional material: the event programme,
the named organization's published profile, a professional project page, and
public venue information. Private contact details, home addresses, relatives,
restricted accounts and ongoing personal movement are outside the assignment.

Write the following before searching:

- Question: the exact claim to establish.
- Purpose: the decision the answer will support.
- Scope: sources, relevant time interval and permitted data.
- Starting evidence: what was supplied and by whom.
- Alternative explanations: at least one plausible way the obvious answer
  could be wrong.
- Completion rule: the evidence needed for a bounded answer.
- Stop rule: when further collection would be irrelevant, unauthorized or too
  uncertain to justify.

For this assignment, completion means explaining the programme attribution
with corroborating professional context and preserving any contrary evidence.
It does not require a complete personal profile. A well-supported partial
answer is more useful than an apparently complete dossier assembled from
unrelated namesakes.

** Search in passes and keep the query history

Start with the most distinctive public-professional detail, not a long bundle
of assumptions. The talk title might distinguish our candidate better than
the common name.

The following queries use fictional domains. The =.example= top-level name is
reserved for documentation; these are exercise strings, not live sources.
[cite:@rfc2606-book]

#+begin_example
"River Sensors" "Harbor Data Forum"
"Alex Rivera" "River Sensors"
site:harbor-forum.example "Alex Rivera"
site:delta-lab.example "River Sensors"
site:harbor-forum.example filetype:pdf programme
"Alex Rivera" "River Sensors" -design
#+end_example

Google documents quoted matching, =site:=, exclusion with =-= and =filetype:=.
It also documents =before:= and =after:= in terms of document updates. Keep
the operator next to its argument. Date-filtered search results are discovery
aids; they do not authenticate when an event happened. [cite:@google-refine-book]

Use a simple search log:

| Pass | Question | Query or source | Observation | Next action |
|------+----------+-----------------+-------------+-------------|
| 1 | Which programme? | talk title plus event name | programme candidate found | open original page |
| 2 | Which namesake? | event site plus full name | two professional contexts | keep separate candidates |
| 3 | Does the organization connect the talk? | organization site plus talk title | reciprocal context found | preserve supporting extract |
| 4 | What could contradict the match? | prior affiliation and dated profile | older biography differs | compare dates and wording |

Open the source behind a result. A snippet may be truncated, stale, or attached
to a page whose context changes its meaning. Record the exact page or document
you examined, rather than treating a search-result card as the evidence itself.

Change one part of a query at a time. If adding the talk title removes the
designer's portfolio from results, that is useful discovery behavior. It does
not prove there are only two Alex Riveras, nor that the remaining result is
correct. Search coverage is limited by indexing, spelling, access and what was
published.

An exclusion term is especially easy to overuse. The =-design= query might
hide a relevant researcher whose profile mentions sensor design. Run the
unfiltered version too, and record why any exclusion was introduced.

A missing result means "not found by these methods in this source set." It
does not mean the subject never held a role, attended an event, or published a
document. Keep that limitation in the final answer.

** Make a source packet before writing the conclusion

For the exercise, imagine that the following short records are supplied.
They are authored premises, not web captures.

| Source | Fictional statement | Time information | Relationship to other sources |
|--------+----------------------+------------------+-------------------------------|
| S1 | The programme lists Alex Rivera, Delta Research Lab, for "River Sensors" | programme dated 12 September | event publisher |
| S2 | Delta's profile links the same talk title and the programme | profile updated 14 September | organization publisher |
| S3 | Alex Rivera's design portfolio lists a different field and organization | portfolio dated 10 September | distinct candidate |
| S4 | A news digest repeats S1 word for word | digest dated 15 September | copied from S1 |
| S5 | An older biography lists candidate A with Coastal Instruments | biography dated 3 February | historical professional context |
| S6 | A venue drawing shows two entrance arches and a square clock | capture time unknown | supplied fictional illustration |

Each source record should retain enough context to answer: what was observed,
where it came from, which version was examined, and what changed during
processing? A practical packet contains:

- a local source identifier;
- the source URL or supplied-artifact identity;
- the publisher and author, if actually available;
- the source's own date statement, including ambiguity;
- the time you retrieved or received it;
- the relevant excerpt and its surrounding context;
- a preserved copy when permitted and useful;
- transformations such as OCR, cropping, translation or normalization;
- access and retention limits;
- claims supported, contradicted, or left unresolved.

The Berkeley Protocol provides a professional framework for preservation and
verification. It distinguishes source attribution from content authentication,
calls for contextual preservation and transformation records, and separates
internal consistency from external corroboration. Its source-analysis guidance
also asks investigators to consider independence and interests.
[cite:@berkeley-protocol-book]

The packet structure here is this book's teaching workflow. It is not a claim
that a particular file format makes evidence court-admissible.

If you compute a content hash, record the algorithm and hash the actual bytes
you retained. A hash can help detect a changed copy; it does not prove who
created the content or whether its claims are true. Do not hash an excerpt and
describe the result as a hash of the whole original document.

Keep originals and transformed working copies distinguishable. An OCR error
in a person's name must not silently replace the preserved spelling. If a date
was interpreted from an ambiguous local format, retain the original text and
record the interpretation separately.

** Resolve identity by competing explanations

Our two fictional Alex Riveras begin as separate candidates.

Candidate A has the river-sensor research context. Candidate B has the design
portfolio context. Their shared name is a search key, not a sufficient merge
key.

Build an evidence matrix around the question:

| Feature | Candidate A | Candidate B | What the feature contributes |
|---------+-------------+-------------+-------------------------------|
| Full name | Alex Rivera | Alex Rivera | no distinction |
| Talk title | linked from professional profile | no link in supplied packet | supports A within this packet |
| Named organization | Delta Research Lab | different design organization | separates the contexts |
| Programme link | reciprocal professional link | none supplied | supports attribution to A |
| Earlier biography | Coastal Instruments | not supplied | possible affiliation change, not immediate disproof |

Do not convert this matrix into an arbitrary numerical identity score. A
weighted sum can hide that all the apparently strong signals came from one
copied biography. Explain which observations distinguish the candidates and
which merely repeat the same assertion.

Try at least three hypotheses:

1. S1 refers to candidate A.
2. S1 refers to candidate B.
3. The packet is insufficient, or S1 contains an attribution error.

S1 and S2 provide coherent professional context for the first hypothesis.
S3 helps explain why a name-only search could confuse the second person with
the first. S4 adds another published copy but no independent reporting chain.
S5 requires temporal interpretation: a February affiliation and a September
programme affiliation may both be accurate for their respective dates.

The next useful question is whether the sources describe different times or
make incompatible claims about the same interval. Avoid deleting a contrary
source simply because it complicates the graph.

A proportionate conclusion is:

#+begin_example
Within the fictional packet, the programme attribution is consistent with
candidate A: the lab profile connects the same talk and programme. Candidate B
remains a separate namesake. The older biography records a different earlier
affiliation; the packet does not establish the person's full employment
history.
#+end_example

This wording exposes the evidence and the limit. It does not silently turn an
event affiliation into verified current employment.

** Count evidence chains rather than copies

Five pages may all repeat one press release. Preserve the copies if they
matter to the question, but keep their dependence visible.

#+begin_example
S1 programme --------------------> C1 programme attribution
   |
   +---- copied by S4 digest ----> repetition of C1

S2 organization profile ---------> contextual corroboration of C1
S3 other namesake ---------------> alternative identity candidate
S5 older biography -------------> time-qualified context / possible tension
#+end_example

Ask what each publisher directly observed, what it received from someone
else, and whether it links or copies that source. Distinct domains are not
automatically independent witnesses. Independent sources can also share an
error, so the final explanation still needs the underlying details.

Record both support and contradiction links. When a finding changes, mark the
old finding superseded or disputed rather than rewriting its history to look
as if you always knew the answer.

Provenance describes the entities, activities and agents involved in producing
information. W3C PROV is useful vocabulary for those relationships; this
chapter's StarIntel examples do not claim PROV serialization or conformance.
[cite:@w3c-prov-book]

** Put only the justified claim into StarIntel

The existing canonical practicum taught =person=, =org=, =source= and =relation=
documents. The fictional packet in =code/osint-evidence/case.json= reuses that
boundary. It contains two person documents, an organization, a synthetic source
record, and one relation.

The relation predicate is =listed-as-speaker-for=. That wording matches the
programme assertion. Using =employed-by= here would strengthen the claim
without adding evidence.

#+begin_src json
{
  "id": "relation:fiction-programme-affiliation",
  "dataset": "book-fictional-osint",
  "dtype": "relation",
  "schemaVersion": "0.10.1",
  "source": {
    "schema": "org.starintel/core@1/person",
    "id": "person:fiction-rivera-science"
  },
  "destination": {
    "schema": "org.starintel/core@1/org",
    "id": "org:fiction-delta-lab"
  },
  "predicate": "listed-as-speaker-for",
  "evidence": [
    {
      "schema": "org.starintel/core@1/source",
      "id": "source:fiction-programme"
    }
  ],
  "verificationStatus": "fictional teaching assertion"
}
#+end_src

The complete fixture adds the synthetic collection context and a plainly
labeled explanation in =confidenceBasis=. It preserves the older-biography
tension as exercise metadata in an extension. That extension is book-local
vocabulary, not a new canonical contract or an implemented query API.

The canonical envelope has provenance and temporal fields, including
=sourceRetrievedAt= and =observedAt=. Choose fields according to what they
mean; do not populate every timestamp from the time an import script ran.
Canonical envelope Unix-time fields use seconds. A source-specific field can
have a different declared type, so consult the generated contract rather than
renaming fields by intuition. [cite:@starlang-starintel-0101]

Schema validation can verify the structure of these documents. It cannot
prove that our fictional source was independently reported, that a person
attended a talk, or that a relationship remains current. The packet tests keep
both person IDs distinct and verify the intended evidence link. They do not
implement an entity-resolution service.

** Location analysis begins by naming the object of the claim

A location statement can refer to several different things:

- the place depicted in an image;
- where a camera was positioned when an image was made;
- the place named by a caption;
- a device's reported position at an acquisition time;
- a person's presence at a particular time;
- a venue's public address.

These claims require different evidence. Identifying a building in a scene
does not identify the photographer. A caption can name a place that is not
visible in the image. A device observation does not establish who was holding
the device.

State the narrow claim before analyzing the material. In this exercise the
claim is "the supplied drawing is consistent with fictional Beacon Hall,"
not "Alex is there now."

** Geolocate a fictional public scene

Our invented venue drawing has these visible features:

1. Two entrance arches.
2. A square clock above the entrance.
3. A low rectangular annex to the right.
4. A canal along the south frontage.

The fictional venue directory supplies two candidates. Beacon Hall has those
four features. Lantern Library has three arches, a round clock, and no annex
on that side.

#+caption: Two wholly fictional public venues for a controlled comparison. The drawing is an original teaching asset and has no real-world coordinates.
#+attr_html: :alt Beacon Hall has two arches, a square clock and an annex on its right; Lantern Library has three arches and a round clock. Labels identify the features used in the exercise.
[[file:assets/fictional-venue-comparison.svg]]

Work from observation to candidate comparison:

1. List visible features without naming a location.
2. Separate durable geometry from changeable decoration.
3. Compare feature combinations rather than one eye-catching resemblance.
4. Record mismatches and possible explanations, such as a different viewpoint
   or a dated renovation record.
5. State the spatial resolution the evidence supports.
6. Preserve an annotated working copy while keeping the supplied original
   distinguishable.

For our controlled drawing, Beacon Hall is the better-supported candidate
because the combined geometry agrees and the library has specific mismatches.
This result is true only within the invented directory. It is not a benchmark
for an image-search system or proof that such features uniquely identify a real
building worldwide.

In public-scene research, an authoritative venue map or dated architectural
image can test a candidate. A generic visual-search match is a lead that still
needs comparison. Work with images you are allowed to analyze and share;
uploading an image to an external search service is itself a disclosure.

Keep location and time findings separate. The scene may be identifiable even
when its capture date is unknown. If a supplied picture was posted today,
today is a publication observation, not automatically its capture date.
Metadata can be missing, altered, or associated with a platform-generated
copy rather than the original file. [cite:@berkeley-protocol-book]

A useful result from S6 is therefore:

#+begin_example
The fictional scene is consistent with Beacon Hall in the supplied directory.
Capture time is unknown. The packet does not establish any person's presence,
camera position outside the depicted scene, or current location.
#+end_example

** Build a timeline with several clocks

For each item, distinguish:

- Event time: when the described event is said to have happened.
- Acquisition time: when a sensor or camera obtained the observation.
- Publication time: when a publisher made an item available.
- Retrieval time: when the researcher obtained this version.
- Analysis time: when the researcher assessed it.

Not every source supplies all five. Leave unknown values unknown.

A timeline entry can preserve an interval rather than a point. If a fictional
newsletter says "during September," do not silently encode 1 September at
midnight as the event time. If a timestamp has no timezone, keep that
ambiguity instead of attaching UTC without evidence.

Put the source and interpretation beside the time. "Programme dated 12
September" and "person attended on 12 September" are different claims. A
schedule can establish an announced plan without proving attendance.

In the same way, an undated professional page retrieved in October does not
prove every statement was current in October. Retrieval refreshes your copy;
it does not refresh the underlying fact.

** Live-location evidence from an opt-in simulation

An active location feed can be useful when participants deliberately share
their own device observations for a defined task. Our exercise imagines an
opt-in coordinator for a fictional public field demonstration. Every fix is
generated data on an invented grid. No device is contacted.

A real browser's Geolocation interface concerns its hosting device and
requires the user's permission. Its timestamp describes acquisition time;
=maximumAge= permits a cached fix. Accuracy is a reported radius in metres,
and the specification does not guarantee the actual device location.
High-accuracy mode is a request, not a promise. These are API semantics, not
proof of personal identity. [cite:@w3c-geolocation-book]

For our teaching system, define the permission before the data:

#+begin_example
Purpose: coordinate the fictional demonstration
Audience: demo-coordinator
Active interval: simulation seconds 900 through 1799
Maximum display age: 60 seconds
Maximum accepted accuracy radius: 30 metres
Revocation: stop display immediately when permission is inactive
Retention: discard demonstration samples when the exercise ends
#+end_example

The interval, thresholds and labels are course choices. They are not universal
safety limits or StarIntel defaults. Real implementations need an authenticated
authorization source, a visible sharing indicator, a clear stop action and an
enforced retention policy. A sample carrying its own "consent=true" field is
not an authority to track its sender.

Now examine this synthetic sample:

#+begin_src json
{
  "observedAtS": 1000,
  "receivedAtS": 1005,
  "accuracyM": 12,
  "gridEastM": 240,
  "gridNorthM": 100
}
#+end_src

The suffix =S= means seconds on the exercise's simulation clock. The grid is
not latitude/longitude. This is a course-local data-transfer object, not a
canonical StarIntel dtype.

At time 1020 the sample is twenty seconds old. At time 1061 it is sixty-one
seconds old and exceeds our display-age limit. Receiving the same old fix
again at 1120 does not make it a new observation.

The classifier in =code/osint-evidence/freshness.py= returns five states:

| State | Meaning in this exercise |
|-------+--------------------------|
| fresh | permission, age and reported accuracy meet the configured display rule |
| stale | the observation is older than the permitted display age |
| coarse | its reported uncertainty exceeds the exercise's threshold |
| blocked | the grant is inactive, expired, not yet active, malformed, or for another audience |
| unknown | clocks or measurement fields cannot support the calculation |

"Fresh" means suitable under that rule. It does not mean a person is certainly
at the point. Do not draw an apparently exact pin when the observation only
supports an uncertain area, or keep a green "live" badge after a feed stops.

The rule uses acquisition time, checks permission before display eligibility,
and fails closed on invalid values. It deliberately refuses to extrapolate a
person's movement from a stale sample. The tests cover cache replay, clock
ordering, expired/revoked permissions, audience mismatch and invalid accuracy.

For a browser integration, convert timestamp units deliberately. W3C's
=EpochTimeStamp= uses milliseconds since the Unix epoch, whereas the canonical
StarIntel common-envelope Unix-time scalar uses seconds. Preserve any useful
fractional precision in an appropriate source record rather than confusing the
two units. [cite:@w3c-hrtime-book;@starlang-starintel-0101]

Stopping acquisition, hiding a display, expiring sharing access and deleting
retained history are separate operations. This small lab implements only the
display classification over supplied data. It does not claim production
authentication, revocation delivery, deletion enforcement or a working
StarIntel tracking service.

The suite has twelve tests. It checks the five canonical documents against

values. It performs no searches, location requests or network communication.

Read a green result precisely: the assertions passed at the tested revision.
It does not certify the quality of a real investigation or a deployed
permission system.

** Report the answer and its limits

A concise investigation result should contain:

1. The question and scope.
2. The strongest supported answer.
3. The sources and the independence of their evidence chains.
4. The competing explanations considered.
5. Material contradictions and how time affects them.
6. What remains unknown.
7. The next bounded action, if one is justified.
8. Retention and sharing restrictions relevant to the recipient.

For the fictional case, the public-professional attribution is supported
within the supplied packet, candidate B remains separate, and S5 is historical
context requiring time-qualified language. The venue comparison favors Beacon
Hall within the invented directory. No capture time or current personal
location is established.

Share the minimum needed for that result. A recipient who needs to confirm a
programme attribution usually needs the programme, matching professional
context and reasoning, not unrelated personal information. If the question is
answered, stop collecting.

** Practice questions and worked answers

1. S1 and S4 agree exactly. Do they provide two independent confirmations?

   No. The packet says S4 copied S1. Preserve the relationship and count the
   underlying source chain once when discussing independence.

2. Candidate A and candidate B share a name. Should their records be merged?

   No. Keep them separate until distinguishing evidence supports identity.
   In this packet the professional contexts point to different candidates.

3. S5 lists an earlier organization. Does it disprove S1?

   Not by itself. Compare the relevant dates and the exact predicates.
   Earlier affiliation and later programme attribution can coexist.

4. The venue drawing matches Beacon Hall. Where was Alex at analysis time?

   Unknown. The supplied drawing does not establish who made it, when it was
   made, or anyone's present location.

5. A source was retrieved today but carries no publication date. What can be
   timestamped confidently?

   The retrieval of that version, if it was actually observed. Publication,
   event and capture times remain separate unknowns.

6. A synthetic fix acquired at 1000 is received again at 1120. With a sixty-
   second age limit, is it fresh at 1120?

   No. Its acquisition age is 120 seconds. A new receipt does not renew it.

7. A sample is ten seconds old and precise, but the participant revoked the
   grant. What should the display classifier return?

   Blocked. Meeting a freshness threshold does not restore authorization.

8. A sample claims an acquisition time later than the current simulation
   clock. Should the application display it as exceptionally fresh?

   No. The temporal evidence is inconsistent. Return unknown and investigate
   clock or input problems without inventing a position.

9. The canonical relation validates. Has the programme affiliation been
   proven true in the real world?

   No. Validation establishes the document contract. Here, all statements are
   explicitly fictional; in real research the sources still require analysis.

10. A dashboard shows no new sample. What is the justified conclusion?

    No new sample has been observed through that path. Device state, network
    state, permission state and a person's behavior remain separate questions.

** Extension exercise

Add a seventh fictional source that genuinely conflicts with S1 about the same
programme version. Keep both source records. Write two possible explanations,
the smallest follow-up question that would distinguish them, and a revised
claim that stays unresolved until that evidence exists.

Then add a test showing that a revoked grant remains blocked even when a new
synthetic sample arrives. This connects the research habit of preserving
contradictions to the engineering habit of making authority and uncertainty
visible at every boundary.
